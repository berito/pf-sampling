\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

A robot that must know where it is has two unreliable sources of information: the controls it was given, which
are corrupted by wheel slip, and its measurements of the surroundings, which are corrupted by sensor noise.
Combining them into an estimate of the pose, with an honest account of its uncertainty, is probabilistic
localization; doing it while building the map is SLAM \cite{thrun2005probabilistic}.

\subsection{The problem as a graphical model}\label{sec:dbn}

Both are naturally written as a dynamic Bayesian network \cite[ch.~6 and 15]{koller2009probabilistic}. The pose
$\x_t$ depends on the previous pose and the control $\uu_t$ through the motion model, and each observation $\z_t$
is conditioned on the pose it was taken from and on the map $\m$ through the measurement model
(\cref{fig:dbn}), so
\begin{equation}
  p(\x_{0:T}, \m, \z_{1:T} \mid \uu_{1:T}) = p(\x_0)\, p(\m) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(\x_t \mid \x_{t-1}, \uu_t)\,
  p(\z_t \mid \x_t, \m).
  \label{eq:joint}
\end{equation}
Controls and observations are observed; the trajectory is latent, and in SLAM the map as well. Correspondences
are known, so each observation carries the identity of the landmark it measures.

What the filter computes is the \emph{belief}, the distribution over the current pose given everything observed
so far. Because $\x_t$ separates past from future it satisfies the Bayes filter recursion, a prediction through
the motion model followed by a correction with the new observation \cite{thrun2005probabilistic}:
\begin{equation}
  p(\x_t \mid \z_{1:t}, \uu_{1:t}) \;\propto\; p(\z_t \mid \x_t)
  \int p(\x_t \mid \x_{t-1}, \uu_t)\, p(\x_{t-1} \mid \z_{1:t-1}, \uu_{1:t-1})\, d\x_{t-1}.
  \label{eq:bayes-filter}
\end{equation}
The state is continuous, so that prediction is an integral. It is tractable in two cases: a discrete state space,
where it is a finite sum, and a linear model with Gaussian noise, where the belief stays Gaussian and the
recursion is the Kalman filter. Range and bearing measurements are nonlinear in the pose, and under global
uncertainty the belief is multimodal, so neither applies and no closed form exists.

\subsection{The particle filter}\label{sec:pf}

Where the integral cannot be carried out it is replaced by samples. The distribution the samples are drawn from
is the \emph{proposal}; drawing a pose from the motion model and weighting it by $p(\z_t \mid \x_t)$ is
likelihood weighting applied to one time slice, with the prior as the proposal
\cite[ch.~12]{koller2009probabilistic}, and carrying the weighted set forward makes it sequential
\cite{gordon1993novel,arulampalam2002tutorial}.

One ingredient has to be added. Over time the weights become products of more and more terms, so they concentrate
on a single particle and the set stops representing the belief; how far this has gone is measured by the
effective sample size $\mathrm{ESS} = 1/\sum_i (\w^{(i)})^2$. Resampling, which replaces the set by $N$ draws
from itself, prevents it, and has no counterpart in the exact algorithm. It costs something in return: drawing
with replacement duplicates the heaviest particles and discards the rest, so the number of distinct particles
falls. \Cref{alg:pf} shows where each decision sits. Lines~5 and~6 are the subject of this report.

\begin{algorithm}[!ht]
\caption{One step of the particle filter. Lines 5 and 6 are what this report varies.}\label{alg:pf}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require particles $\{\x_{t-1}^{(i)}, \w_{t-1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}$, control $\uu_t$, observation $\z_t$
\For{$i = 1 \dots N$}
  \State $\x_t^{(i)} \sim q(\x \mid \x_{t-1}^{(i)}, \uu_t, \z_t)$
         \Comment{\emph{proposal}; the motion model throughout this report}
  \State $\tilde{\w}_t^{(i)} \gets \w_{t-1}^{(i)}\, p(\z_t \mid \x_t^{(i)})$
         \Comment{weight by the new observation}
\EndFor
\State $\w_t^{(i)} \gets \tilde{\w}_t^{(i)} / \sum_j \tilde{\w}_t^{(j)}$
\If{$\textsc{Trigger}(\w_t)$}                \Comment{\emph{when to resample}}
  \State $\{a_i\} \gets \textsc{Resample}(\w_t)$   \Comment{\emph{which scheme}: $N$ indices drawn from $\w_t$}
  \State $\x_t^{(i)} \gets \x_t^{(a_i)}$, \quad $\w_t^{(i)} \gets 1/N$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\{\x_t^{(i)}, \w_t^{(i)}\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Rao-Blackwellization and FastSLAM}\label{sec:rbpf}

In SLAM the state is the pose together with the map, so the state space gains two dimensions per landmark and
sampling it directly is intractable. The network offers a way out. An observation of landmark $\m_k$ has exactly
two parents, the pose it was taken from and $\m_k$ itself, so any path between two landmarks must pass through
an observation's pose with both edges pointing away from it; a known trajectory blocks every such path. The
landmarks are d-separated given the trajectory \cite{koller2009probabilistic}, and the posterior splits:
\begin{equation}
  p(\x_{1:t}, \m \mid \z_{1:t}, \uu_{1:t}) = p(\x_{1:t} \mid \z_{1:t}, \uu_{1:t})
  \prod_{k=1}^{K} p(\m_k \mid \x_{1:t}, \z_{1:t}).
  \label{eq:rao-blackwell}
\end{equation}
Sampling only the first factor and solving the rest exactly is Rao-Blackwellization \cite{doucet2000rao}, and
FastSLAM applies it by giving every particle one trajectory and one extended Kalman filter per landmark
\cite{montemerlo2002fastslam}. Only the trajectory is sampled, which is what makes particle-filter SLAM
affordable.

\subsection{Learning the parameters}\label{sec:learning}

The structure of \eqref{eq:joint} fixes which distributions appear, not the numbers inside them; write those as
$\theta$. Estimating them from data is \emph{parameter} learning rather than structure learning, and here it is
parameter learning \emph{with incomplete data} \cite[ch.~19]{koller2009probabilistic}, because the trajectory is
latent. The network is also a template model, so the same conditional distribution is used at every slice and its
parameters are tied across slices \cite[ch.~6]{koller2009probabilistic}: every step is evidence about the same
$\theta$, and a longer trajectory is more data rather than more unknowns.

The estimate is the $\theta$ that makes the observations most probable, $\hat{\theta} = \arg\max_\theta \log
p(\z_{1:T} \mid \uu_{1:T}, \theta)$, and that maximum is in the right place: the average log likelihood tends to
a term free of $\theta$ minus the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the true distribution and the model, which
vanishes only where the two agree \cite[ch.~17]{koller2009probabilistic}. The likelihood factorizes into one-step
terms, each an integral with no closed form, but line~3 of \cref{alg:pf} already computes what is needed:
\begin{equation}
  p(\z_t \mid \z_{1:t-1}, \uu_{1:t}, \theta) \;\approx\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{\w}_t^{(i)},
  \label{eq:evidence}
\end{equation}
the sum the filter forms in order to normalize \cite{doucet2011tutorial}. Two consequences follow. This estimate
of the likelihood is unbiased, so the estimate of its logarithm is biased downwards, and the bias grows as the
particle approximation degrades. And the curvature of the expected score is proportional to the number of steps,
so a parameter the observations say little about leaves a flat likelihood, and a longer trajectory sharpens it.
