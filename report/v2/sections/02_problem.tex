\section{Problem statement and objectives}\label{sec:problem}

The structure of the model is fixed in advance by the robot's kinematics and its sensor geometry, so there is no
structure to learn. What is left is the inference: the belief cannot be computed in closed form, so it is
approximated by a set of samples, and the estimate is only as good as that set. How that set is proposed, weighted and resampled is the
first thing measured here.

The motion model and the measurement model each carry a noise level, and in practice these are almost always set
by hand. In this project I estimate them instead, from the data the robot itself produced. That is the second
thing measured here, and the weights the filter already computes are what make it possible.

\paragraph{Sampling.} A filter must choose a proposal distribution, a rule for when to resample, a resampling
scheme and a number of particles, and these are usually inherited from whatever implementation is at hand. I
built a testbed in which they are interchangeable parts of one filter loop, so that one can be varied while the
world, the trajectory and the random seed stay fixed. The filters themselves are existing open-source
implementations: the localization filters and resampling schemes of Elfring et al.\ \cite{elfring2021particle},
and FastSLAM 1.0 from PythonRobotics \cite{sakai2018pythonrobotics}.

Two of the four are the subject of this report, lines~6 and~5 of \cref{alg:pf}. The other two are held fixed so
that they cannot confound the comparison: the proposal is always the motion model, which draws from the prior,
and the number of particles is fixed within each comparison.

\paragraph{The resampling scheme} decides how the $N$ new indices are drawn from the weights. All four are
unbiased, in that a particle of weight $\w^{(i)}$ is expected to be copied $N\w^{(i)}$ times; they differ in how
much that count varies around its expectation, and therefore in how many distinct particles survive
\cite{douc2005comparison,hol2006resampling}. Each is drawn below taking $N = 6$ draws from the same six weights,
in the colour it keeps in every result figure; a particle is copied once for each tick that lands on it.

\emph{Multinomial} takes $N$ independent draws from the weights, so the number of copies of a particle is
binomial. This has the largest variance of the four: the draws clump, and particles are missed.
\strip{\foreach \x in {0.067, 0.103, 0.155, 0.236, 0.396, 0.402}
        \draw[very thick, schemeMultinomial] (\x,-0.05) -- (\x,0.75);}

\emph{Residual} first takes $\lfloor N \w^{(i)} \rfloor$ copies of each particle outright (solid), then fills the
remaining places multinomially from the leftover weight (dashed). Removing the whole part removes most of the
randomness.
\strip{\foreach \x in {0.15, 0.42, 0.65} \draw[very thick, schemeResidual] (\x,-0.05) -- (\x,0.75);
       \foreach \x in {0.08, 0.34, 0.80}
        \draw[very thick, schemeResidual, densely dashed] (\x,-0.05) -- (\x,0.75);}

\emph{Stratified} cuts $[0,1)$ into $N$ equal strata (dotted) and draws one uniform number inside each, forcing
the draws to spread over the cumulative weights.
\strip{\foreach \j in {1,2,3,4,5} \draw[schemeStratified!55, densely dotted] ({\j/6},-0.09) -- ({\j/6},0.79);
       \foreach \x in {0.153, 0.3, 0.461, 0.537, 0.756, 0.879}
        \draw[very thick, schemeStratified] (\x,-0.05) -- (\x,0.75);}

\emph{Systematic} draws a single uniform $u \in [0, 1/N)$ and takes the $N$ positions $u + (j-1)/N$. One random
number gives the lowest variance of the four, at the cost of correlating the draws.
\strip{\foreach \j in {0,1,2,3,4,5}
        \draw[very thick, schemeSystematic] ({0.07+\j/6},-0.05) -- ({0.07+\j/6},0.75);}

\paragraph{When to resample} decides whether line~6 runs at all on a given step. Resampling fights degeneracy but
discards particles, so a rule has to balance the two.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Every step}, and \emph{never}, as the two extremes.
  \item \emph{An ESS threshold}: resample when the effective sample size falls below a fraction of $N$, tested at
        0.2, 0.5 and 0.8. This measures degeneracy directly.
  \item \emph{A largest-weight threshold}: resample when one particle's weight rises above 0.1, 0.2 or 0.5. This
        reacts to a single particle taking over, which the ESS notices more slowly.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Parameter learning.} The noise values are parameters of the network's two conditional distributions,
and the weights the filter already computes estimate the marginal likelihood of the observations under them,
so they can be estimated from a recorded run instead of assumed. This asks not how well
the filter infers under a given model, but which parts of the model the data can determine at all.
