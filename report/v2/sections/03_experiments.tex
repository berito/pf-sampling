\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Method}\label{sec:method}

The four sampling choices are interchangeable components of a single filter loop, each selected by name, so a
configuration is a set of names and a particle count. The same loop serves both problems; what differs is the
state a particle carries, a pose in localization and a pose with its own map in SLAM.

Two simulated worlds are used, both taken unchanged from the reference implementations. The \emph{localization
world} is a \SI{10}{\meter} square, cyclic at its edges, with four landmarks. The robot takes 30 steps of
\SI{0.25}{\meter} and \SI{0.02}{\radian}, with motion noise of \SI{0.005}{\meter} and \SI{0.002}{\radian}, and
measures range and bearing to all four landmarks with noise of \SI{0.2}{\meter} and \SI{0.05}{\radian}.
Particles start uniformly over the world and all headings, so the opening steps are global localization; the
learning experiments add a setting in which they start around the true initial pose, so the filter tracks
instead. The \emph{SLAM world} is the FastSLAM scenario of its reference implementation: 500 steps among eight
landmarks, observations within \SI{20}{\meter} with noise of \SI{0.3}{\meter} and \SI{2}{\degree}, and controls
corrupted by noise and a constant yaw-rate offset.

A run is one configuration on one seed, and the seed fixes the world, the trajectory and the filter's randomness.
The same seeds are used for every variant in a comparison, so two variants differ only in the choice being
varied. Every configuration is run on twenty seeds, and every table reports the mean and standard deviation over
them. A difference is called clear when the 95\% confidence intervals of the two means do not overlap, and it is
only taken between variants sharing the value of the other varied setting.

What is measured: accuracy, as position RMSE and as ATE after the best rigid alignment, plus map error in SLAM;
degeneracy, as the mean effective sample size and the fraction of distinct particles surviving a resampling step;
consistency, as the median NEES, about 3 when the particle spread matches the error; cost, as runtime per step;
and fit, as the log likelihood \eqref{eq:evidence} of the observations. Runtime compares implementations rather
than schemes: three of the four reused resamplers search the cumulative weights in a way that costs $O(N^2)$,
where all four can be written in $O(N)$, so they differ by an order of magnitude for reasons that have nothing
to do with the sampling.

The filters are the reference implementations, adapted where the experiments require it: the sampling steps are
made swappable, the models are vectorized over particles and moved to log weights, and the code is checked
against the originals. The adapted code is in the repository.

\subsection{Sampling}\label{sec:results-sampling}

Two experiments, each varying one sampling choice alone. The proposal is the motion model throughout and the
number of particles is fixed within each comparison, so neither can confound the result.

\subsubsection{Resampling scheme}

The four schemes at 50, 200 and 1000 particles in localization, resampling at every step so that only the scheme
differs (240 runs), and the same four in FastSLAM 1.0 at 10 and 50 particles (160 runs).

In localization the four are equally accurate: at every particle count their errors differ by less than the
seed-to-seed spread. They differ clearly in how many distinct particles survive a resampling step, in the order
their variance predicts: with 1000 particles systematic keeps 72\% distinct, stratified 67\%, residual 65\% and
multinomial 54\%, and the same order holds at 50 and 200.

That difference reaches the estimate in one place. In FastSLAM a particle that is lost takes its map with it, so
with 10 particles systematic gives an ATE of \SI{0.23}{\meter} against \SI{0.32}{\meter} for multinomial, and a
map error of \SI{0.105}{\meter} against \SI{0.139}{\meter}.

\subsubsection{When to resample}

Eight rules in localization with 100 particles and systematic resampling: at every step, never, an ESS threshold
of 0.2, 0.5 or 0.8, and a largest-weight threshold of 0.1, 0.2 or 0.5 (160 runs).

Whether the filter resamples is what matters. Not resampling triples the error, \SI{1.39}{\meter} against
roughly \SI{0.46}{\meter}, and leaves the effective sample size at 1\% of $N$: with 100 particles a single one
carries the belief, and the filter is confidently wrong rather than merely inaccurate. Every rule that does
resample gives the same error, between \SI{0.45}{\meter} and \SI{0.49}{\meter}.

What the threshold changes is cost against impoverishment. It sets how often resampling happens, between a third
of the steps and all of them, and resampling less often leaves each step with more degenerate weights, so fewer
particles survive the redraw: 25\% at a third of the steps against 64\% at every step.

\subsection{Learning the noise}\label{sec:results-learning}

Each of the four noise parameters is swept in turn while the other three are held at the values the world uses,
and every value is scored by the log likelihood \eqref{eq:evidence} of the recorded measurements. The filter uses
the motion-model proposal, systematic resampling, an ESS threshold of 0.5 and 2000 particles, and starts around
the true initial pose (640 runs).

\subsubsection{Is learning better than setting the noise by hand?}

For the measurement noise, yes. \Cref{tab:learned} puts the three values side by side: the hand-set range noise
is twice what the world uses and the hand-set bearing noise six times it, while the learned values are within
5\% of the truth. The filter is more accurate at the learned setting, \SI{0.063}{\meter} against
\SI{0.071}{\meter}, about 11\% better. The gain is modest because a filter is forgiving of noise set too wide.

For the motion noise, no. Its sweeps are flat, the log likelihood holding at 205 to 206 while the assumed noise
varies by a factor of 30, so there is no learned value to put in place of the hand-set one. The true values are
small next to the measurement noise the filter corrects with at every step, so the observations barely depend on
them.

Which estimates to trust can be decided without knowing the truth, by asking whether separate recordings agree.
Each of the twenty seeds is an independent recording, and the last column of \cref{tab:learned} counts how many
land on the same value: all twenty for the bearing noise, seventeen for the range noise, while the motion
estimates scatter across the whole sweep. A parameter the data pins down gives the same answer from every
recording; one it does not gives a different answer each time.

The shape of the peak is the one the model predicts. Too little assumed noise makes the measurements that
arrived look impossible and the score falls steeply, from 205 at \SI{0.19}{\meter} to $-252$ at
\SI{0.08}{\meter}; too much makes every pose explain them equally well and the score decays slowly, to 98 at
\SI{0.8}{\meter}. For a Gaussian residual of true width $\sigma^*$ scored under $\sigma$ the expected score is
$-\log\sigma - \sigma^{*2}/2\sigma^2$ up to a constant, maximized at $\sigma^*$ but falling like $\sigma^{-2}$
below it and only like $\log\sigma$ above. With 120 range residuals that expression predicts the whole measured
curve with nothing fitted: at 0.142, 0.253, 0.45 and \SI{0.8}{\meter} it gives $-18$, $-6$, $-49$ and $-110$
relative to the peak, against measured $-20$, $-5$, $-47$ and $-108$. It parts company only at the far left,
predicting $-205$ where $-457$ is measured, because there the weights degenerate and the estimator's downward
bias takes over.

\subsubsection{What learning requires}

Three further sets of runs, each changing one condition: the turn sweep on a 400-step trajectory instead of 30
(140 runs); the range sweep from the uniform start as well as from a known pose (360 runs); and the range sweep
at 25 to 2000 particles, and under each resampling scheme at 200 particles (1440 runs).

Learning needs enough data, a filter that is already tracking, and enough particles. The resampling scheme makes
no difference.

On 400 steps the flat turn curve gains a maximum at the true value, and an assumed drift ten times too small is
rejected by thousands of nats; the twenty recordings concentrate instead of scattering. Identifiability is a
property of the data, not of the method: because the parameters are tied across time slices, the curvature of
the likelihood grows with the number of steps, as \cref{sec:background} predicts.

From the uniform start the maximum disappears altogether. With the true, small noise the filter never finds the
robot within 30 steps: the position error is \SI{2.3}{\meter} against \SI{0.063}{\meter} from a known start, and
the log likelihood is four orders of magnitude lower. The score then simply rises with wider assumed noise,
because wider noise lets the particles spread enough to be useful, and only 2 of 20 recordings agree. What the
likelihood reports in this regime is which setting lets this filter work, not which setting generated the data.

At the true value the estimated log likelihood rises from $28 \pm 316$ with 25 particles to $205 \pm 13$ with
2000, and agreement rises with it, from no two recordings agreeing to 17 of 20: both the bias and the spread
shrink as the approximation improves. Under the four resampling schemes at 200 particles the answer is the same
throughout, \SI{0.19}{\meter}, with 14 or 15 of 20 recordings agreeing.
