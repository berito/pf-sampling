\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Which choices mattered.} The number of particles and the decision to resample at all had by far the largest
effects on accuracy. Going from 10 to 500 particles reduced the localization error almost tenfold, and a filter that never
resamples was three times worse than any filter that does. The choices within resampling mattered much less. In
localization the four schemes and seven rules were equally accurate. The schemes differed in how many distinct
particles survive, with the known order from multinomial to systematic resampling
\cite{douc2005comparison,hol2006resampling}, and the rules differed in how often they resample. These differences
became visible in accuracy only in FastSLAM with 10 particles, where losing a particle means losing a whole map
hypothesis. The proposal mattered in between: a good measurement-informed proposal, the auxiliary particle filter in
localization and FastSLAM 2.0 in SLAM, helped most when particles are scarce, and its advantage shrank or vanished with
more particles.

\paragraph{Trade-offs.} Measurement-informed proposals cost more per particle, so the fair comparison is at equal
runtime rather than at equal particle count. At about 0.6\,ms per step, the auxiliary particle filter with 100 particles
reached 0.26\,m and the motion model with 300 particles 0.30\,m, a difference within the seed-to-seed spread; the
auxiliary filter's clear advantage is at equal particle count. FastSLAM 2.0 with 5 to 10 particles and FastSLAM 1.0
with 25 particles built maps of similar quality in similar time. Adaptive resampling brings no gain in accuracy in these worlds, but it saves
resampling steps at no loss. Resampling less often leaves each resampling step with more degenerate weights, and
therefore with fewer distinct particles afterwards, so the threshold trades cost against impoverishment per step.

\paragraph{The extended Kalman particle filter.} The extended Kalman particle filter gave the lowest effective sample size
of all proposals and, with many particles, a higher error than the motion model. A measurement-informed proposal is
meant to approximate $p(\x_t \mid \x_{t-1}, \z_t, \uu_t)$ so that the weights \eqref{eq:weight} become more even. A likely
reason why it does not here lies in the form used: each particle's Kalman covariance is carried from step to step and
grows with the accumulated uncertainty of the filter, while the weights compare the sample with a single motion step. When the proposal is much
wider or differently shaped than the posterior of one step, most samples receive small weights. Linearizing the
measurement model about each particle's own pose, far from the true pose after the uniform start, adds to this. A
proposal built from the motion noise of one step alone, the linearized form of the optimal proposal
\cite{doucet2011tutorial}, is the natural alternative and a candidate for further experiments.

\paragraph{The graphical-model view.} The experiments can be read as a study of inference design choices on a dynamic
Bayesian network. The proposal decides how much of the evidence $\z_t$ enters the sampling step and how much is left
to the weights: the motion-model proposal uses only the parent $\x_{t-1}$ of $\x_t$, while the measurement-informed
proposals also condition on its child $\z_t$. Rao-Blackwellization decides which variables are sampled at all. In
FastSLAM only the trajectory is sampled and the map is handled exactly given it, which is why a handful of particles
already produces a usable map. Resampling has no counterpart in exact inference. It is the price of representing a
belief by a finite sample, and its variants only differ in how much extra noise they add while paying it.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The worlds are small and simulated: a 30-step localization run in a $10 \times 10$\,m world
with four landmarks, and one SLAM route with eight landmarks and known correspondences. Real sensors, unknown data
association and larger maps would make degeneracy and proposal quality more important. The filter noise settings are
those of the upstream code, and they are larger than the true noise; filters tuned closer to the true noise would have
more peaked likelihoods and more degenerate weights, which could change the ranking of the proposals and rules. Each
experiment varies one choice with the others fixed, so interactions, for example between the resampling scheme and the
rule for when to resample, are not measured. The rules for when to resample were compared only in localization, the
resampling schemes in SLAM only with FastSLAM 1.0, and the auxiliary and extended Kalman proposals only in localization.
Runtimes were measured with 16 runs in parallel and depend on the reused implementations, as for the resampling
schemes. MCMC moves after resampling \cite{gilks2001following}, which address impoverishment directly, were not
evaluated.
